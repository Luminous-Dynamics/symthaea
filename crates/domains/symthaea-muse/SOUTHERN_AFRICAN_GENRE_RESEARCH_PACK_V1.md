# Southern African genre research pack v1

**Status:** source-grounded research intake; not a runtime capability claim, not a claim of cultural authenticity, and not a completed musician review.

**Parent taxonomy proposal:** [PR #7418](https://github.com/Luminous-Dynamics/symthaea/pull/7418) (open, not merged). The IDs below refer to that proposal and are not yet present on current `main`.

**Catalog scope:** proposed IDs 153–160 in PR #7418, all currently `ResearchExpertReviewRequired`, all with `composer_style: None`. This document does not change those statuses, add routes, or promote any style. It is based on current `main` and does not import the older taxonomy branch.

## 1. Why this pack exists

The eight Southern African identities should not be represented as one generic “township” sound. The evidence describes different musical organizations, periods, performance settings, and production practices:

- Maskandi is a guitar-centred Zulu song practice whose introductory `isihlabo` and later guitar/vocal interplay are structurally important.
- Isicathamiya is a male-voice a cappella choral and competitive performance tradition, not an instrumental folk preset.
- Marabi is an urban, improvisatory dance tradition built around cyclical accompaniment; it is an important historical ancestor, not a synonym for every later township style.
- Sophiatown/African Jazz, kwela, and mbaqanga have related but distinguishable histories and rhythmic/ensemble identities.
- Bubblegum and kwaito are later Black South African popular forms with electronic and international influences, but different historical periods and different approaches to vocals, groove, and production.

The point of a genre record is to preserve a scoped identity and the evidence supporting it. It must not force every tradition into the same composer schema. A vocal competition tradition, a guitar song practice, an urban dance cycle, and an electronically produced pop idiom need different realization contracts.

## 2. Research boundary and evidence vocabulary

This is a first-pass source pack, not a finished ethnomusicological review. The sources are strongest for historical context and selected musical characteristics; they do not establish a complete grammar for every period, region, ensemble, or community.

Use these evidence labels:

- **Grounded observation:** directly described by a cited study, archival account, or institutional source.
- **Candidate discriminator:** a potentially useful feature to test against representative recordings/scores and counterexamples. It is not yet a validated rule.
- **Open question:** a point requiring more sources, listening analysis, or practitioner review.
- **Not inferred:** an attribute Melothaea must not invent from the genre name alone.

Do not promote any of these records to executable or Foundation status on the strength of this document. A source-backed hypothesis is not a composer route; a passing symbolic test is not evidence of authentic performance.

## 3. Eight identities: working research profiles

### 153 — Kwaito

**Scope to investigate:** Black South African electronic/popular dance music emerging in the 1990s, particularly in relation to township youth culture and local engagements with house and other international sounds. Keep early/mid-1990s Kwaito distinguishable from later evolutions and from house, hip-hop, gqom, and amapiano.

**Grounded observations**
- Gavin Steingo treats Kwaito as an electronic music that emerged alongside South Africa's mid-1990s democratization and examines its aesthetic and social meanings rather than reducing it to an imported house subgenre ([*Kwaito's Promise*](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226362687.001.0001), University of Chicago Press, 2016).
- South African History Online traces a complex set of influences and predecessors, including marabi, kwela, mbaqanga, Maskandi, 1980s bubblegum, praise poetry, hip-hop, dub, jazz, and UK house ([history of Kwaito](https://sahistory.org.za/article/history-kwaito-music)).
- Dion Eaby-Lomas emphasizes ambiguous, non-linear histories among Kwaito, bubblegum, mbaqanga, and later amapiano; the article is useful precisely because it warns against a single uncontested origin story ([“Kwaito as history”](https://doi.org/10.21504/amj.v12i1.2529), *African Music*, 2023).
- A detailed account of early Kwaito describes vocal delivery ranging from rhythmic speech to song, with slower, repeated phrases/catchwords in contrast to some US house practices; this should be checked against the cited ethnographic work and specific recordings before being encoded as a hard rule ([Steingo, *Kwaito's Promise*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/K/bo23290913.html)).

**Candidate discriminators to test**
- Repeated, loop-oriented dance foundations with a local rhythmic and production identity.
- A broad vocal continuum: rhythmic speech, chant, sung refrain, and call/response; do not force one vocal mode.
- Arrangement and production should be period-conditioned: early Kwaito cannot be modeled by simply selecting a contemporary house drum kit or an amapiano log-drum palette.

**Not inferred**
- No universal BPM, drum pattern, chord loop, language, lyric topic, or vocal delivery.
- Do not make “Kwaito” synonymous with South African house, gqom, or amapiano.
- Do not assign a generic political or lyrical stance.

**Next evidence gate:** define period/scene subprofiles; select a rights-cleared representative listening set; compare rhythmic/vocal/production features against South African house, hip-hop, bubblegum, and gqom controls; seek producer, DJ, vocalist, and listener review.

### 154 — Maskandi

**Scope to investigate:** Zulu Maskandi/Maskanda song traditions and their changing solo and ensemble practices. Retain spelling and terminology variants as aliases with source provenance rather than declaring one universal spelling.

**Grounded observations**
- Ntombela's study of Zulu Maskandi songs finds the `izihlabo` instrumental introduction to be a predominant and obligatory basis for the song, while documenting varied introductions using concertina, bass guitar, voices, lead guitar, and leader/group interplay (`ukubiza ingoma`) ([Ntombela, 2017](https://journals.co.za/doi/10.25159/1016-8427/1465)).
- Research on Maskanda describes an unmetered solo-guitar `isihlabo` that lays out harmonic material, followed by a melodic-rhythmic guitar pattern and overlapping call-and-response between singer, guitar lines, and/or backing singers ([“Something else is possible,” 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S026114302100043X)).
- Kathryn Olsen's ethnomusicological study describes Maskanda as distinguished by fast guitar picking and blues-inflected vocal intonation, with a history among Zulu migrant workers ([*Music and Social Change in South Africa: Maskanda Past and Present*, 2014](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt14btb8n)).

**Candidate discriminators to test**
- A free/unmetered introductory guitar statement followed by a transition into a more regular accompaniment pattern.
- Guitar as a source of melody, pulse, harmonic implication, and response—not only chordal accompaniment.
- Vocal/guitar overlap and response timing; test different regional, historical, and ensemble variants.
- Picked figures, ornamentation, and guitar-register behavior as measurable identity candidates.

**Not inferred**
- Do not assume every Maskandi performance uses one fixed guitar tuning, tempo, scale, lyric subject, or ensemble.
- Do not synthesize `izihlabo` as a generic metronomic four-bar intro.
- Do not infer authenticity from adding a concertina or a Zulu-language vocal.

**Next evidence gate:** compare solo and group examples; document guitar tuning/technique and intro-to-song transition; include contemporary production variants; obtain review from Maskandi musicians and knowledgeable listeners.

### 155 — Isicathamiya

**Scope to investigate:** Zulu a cappella choral and performance practice, especially its historically important male-voice ensemble and all-night competition contexts. Keep Isicathamiya distinct from broader Zulu vocal music, church hymnody, and generic barbershop or Western choral arrangements.

**Grounded observations**
- Ndlovu's 1996 Rhodes University music dissertation studies Isicathamiya as a Zulu male vocal tradition performed by migrant workers in all-night competitions, with links among performance structure, social practice, custom, and choreography ([repository record](https://hdl.handle.net/20.500.14915/10338)).
- Veit Erlmann's *Nightsong* is a comprehensive ethnomusicological study of Isicathamiya performance practice, history, migrant-worker choir culture, dance, dress, praise, and competition ([University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/N/bo3632138.html)).
- A 2022 study describes Isicathamiya as a cappella male choral music that developed in Natal in the 1920s–1930s and later in Johannesburg, while stressing its relation to Ingoma, migrant labour, and Zulu performance contexts ([SciELO article](https://www.scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S0259-94222022000300022&script=sci_arttext)).
- South African Department of Basic Education material describes close/cyclic harmony, deep bass, leader/group call-and-response, isiZulu texts, vocal crepitation/ululation, and choreography; use this as an educational summary, subordinate to ethnomusicological research ([DBE Music study guide](https://www.education.gov.za/Portals/0/Documents/Manuals/2022%20Study%20Guides/MUSIC%20.pdf?ver=2022-09-07-102740-933)).

**Candidate discriminators to test**
- A cappella multi-part voice-leading with a bass foundation and upper-part chordal/contrapuntal response.
- Lead/choir alternation, close-harmony movement, phrase-ending coordination, and controlled vocal attacks/releases.
- Performance pacing and choreography as part of a complete performance identity, not just a MIDI note pattern.

**Not inferred**
- Do not equate it with generic SATB chorale, barbershop, or unaccompanied hymn singing.
- Do not make all performances male-only by schema fiat: the historically dominant male-voice profile needs to be represented without erasing other vocal practices or contemporary variation.
- Do not reduce the tradition's social/competitive practice to a purely acoustic timbre preset.

**Next evidence gate:** engage choir leaders and singers; separate choral score grammar, vocal production, competition sequencing, and movement; build a listening protocol that evaluates ensemble blend and phrasing, not only pitch accuracy.

### 156 — Mbaqanga

**Scope to investigate:** Black South African urban popular/dance styles using the historically specific term Mbaqanga. Because the name has been used for more than one historical development, every profile must declare period, scene, and source usage.

**Grounded observations**
- Louise Meintjes' studio ethnography describes the distinctive Mbaqanga sound associated with Mahlathini and the Mahotella Queens: a bass solo voice, soaring female-frontline harmonies, electric guitar, bass, keyboard, and drum set ([*Sound of Africa!*, Duke University Press, 2003](https://www.dukeupress.edu/sound-of-africa)).
- A study of musical exchange describes the 1960s Mbaqanga cycle, short repeated/varied melodies, solo/chorus call-and-response, driving straight beat, independent instrumental lines, and electric guitar/bass as distinguishing features ([“Dancing to the beat of the diaspora,” DOI 10.1080/17528631.2010.481976](https://doi.org/10.1080/17528631.2010.481976)).
- South African DBE guidance identifies a four-bar harmonic cycle, close four-part vocal harmony, strong bass, indlamu-derived drum impulse, repeated motifs, and layered/polyrhythmic activity ([DBE 2023 Music marking guideline](https://www.education.gov.za/Portals/0/CD/2023%20NovPapers/Non-Languages%20Nov%202023%20MG%20PDF/Music/Music%20P1%20Nov%202023%20MG%20Eng.pdf?ver=2024-02-23-123545-000)).
- Eaby-Lomas places Mbaqanga in a longer, contested history of Black township dance music, rather than a simple linear precursor to later genres ([“Kwaito as history,” 2023](https://doi.org/10.21504/amj.v12i1.2529)).

**Candidate discriminators to test**
- Strong, dance-oriented straight pulse, with bass and drums carrying more than a generic root-note foundation.
- Interlocking independent melodic lines, particularly bass and lead guitar.
- Repeated short melodic material organized around a cyclic harmonic frame.
- Lead/bass vocal and backing-group interaction; close harmony as a specific ensemble arrangement, not simply “choir”.

**Not inferred**
- Do not reduce all Mbaqanga to one four-chord loop or one Mahotella Queens arrangement.
- Do not confuse the 1940s “African Jazz” naming history with the later studio/dance Mbaqanga sound without declaring the period and usage.

**Next evidence gate:** construct period-specific subprofiles (at minimum early/1960s–70s and later studio/ensemble practice); compare bass/guitar independence and vocal arrangement; review with Mbaqanga musicians and historians.

### 157 — Kwela

**Scope to investigate:** South African pennywhistle-led popular music, especially the recordings and performance practices of the 1950s and early 1960s. Keep the pennywhistle-led tradition distinct from later sax-jive and from the general label “jive”.

**Grounded observations**
- Lara Allen's University of Natal M.Mus thesis provides detailed analysis of more than 250 compositions identified as Kwela, covering form, harmony, melody, rhythm, instrumentation, pennywhistle technique, guitar, banjo, bass, drums, and saxophone ([*Pennywhistle Kwela*, 1993](https://hdl.handle.net/10413/9106); [full thesis](https://researchspace.ukzn.ac.za/bitstreams/58dd0dba-5a1e-4328-9854-fa7a35155695/download)).
- Allen distinguishes swung and straight-beat Kwela repertories: swing subdivision reflects jazz roots, while straight-beat examples point toward neo-traditional tendencies that also contributed to later Mbaqanga. Banjo/guitar shuffle and triplet figures have documented interlocking functions.
- South African DBE material records cyclic chord progressions, solo-led ensembles, moderate/upbeat tempos, skiffle-like beat, jive/swing rhythms, secondary melodic lines, and improvisation ([DBE 2017 Music marking guideline](https://www.education.gov.za/LinkClick.aspx?fileticket=QrYV0rGV13o%3D&forcedownload=true&mid=7353&portalid=0&tabid=1856)).
- The thesis describes Kwela as a historically bounded and internally varied musical practice, not merely “pennywhistle over a shuffle”.

**Candidate discriminators to test**
- Lead pennywhistle (or historically situated saxophone successor) with melodic improvisation and ornamentation.
- Cyclic accompaniment with an explicitly selected straight or swung subdivision.
- Interlocking banjo/guitar rhythm, bass, and drum parts; secondary melodic lines.
- Phrase-level call/response and solo-to-ensemble relationships where present.

**Not inferred**
- Do not force every Kwela piece into swing; the corpus contains straight-beat examples too.
- Do not assume a pennywhistle is always present in later variants or that one instrument alone proves Kwela identity.

**Next evidence gate:** encode separate straight/swing rhythm hypotheses and front-line variants; validate against Allen's analytical corpus descriptions and rights-cleared audio; ask pennywhistle and band practitioners to assess idiomatic phrasing.

### 158 — Marabi

**Scope to investigate:** early urban Black South African dance music associated with Johannesburg slumyards and shebeen culture in the 1920s–1930s, alongside later historical uses of the label. Do not use “Marabi” as a synonym for all cyclic African popular music.

**Grounded observations**
- Christopher Ballantine's peer-reviewed article describes Marabi as a keyboard/banjo/guitar style based on a cyclic harmonic pattern; the basic cycle spans four measures, commonly I–IV–I6/4–V. Its melodies are placed over an indefinitely repeating accompaniment, with improvisatory performance practice ([“Concert and Dance,” *Popular Music*, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261143000004475)).
- South African History Online locates Marabi in Johannesburg slumyard/shebeen culture, especially Doornfontein, and describes its piano/organ-centred, original and improvisational sound ([“Music and culture as forms of resistance”](https://sahistory.org.za/article/music-and-culture-forms-resistance)).
- Lara Allen's historical work places Marabi among the urban styles that preceded Kwela and influenced subsequent musical developments ([*Pennywhistle Kwela*, 1993](https://hdl.handle.net/10413/9106)).

**Candidate discriminators to test**
- Repeating multi-bar harmonic cycle with flexible melodic improvisation above it.
- Keyboard/organ/piano-led texture with guitar/banjo variants.
- Long-form dance performance sustained through repetition, variation, and performer interaction rather than conventional verse/chorus development.

**Not inferred**
- Do not hardcode one progression as universal; I–IV–I6/4–V is a documented common/basic pattern, not a complete definition.
- Do not assign a generic “African scale”, percussion pattern, or contemporary production palette solely from the name.

**Next evidence gate:** compare historical accounts, surviving transcriptions, and later revival/reinterpretation; define a period boundary and identify how melody, accompaniment, and improvisation interact in recorded examples.

### 159 — Township Jazz (name review required)

**Scope to investigate:** the current label is too broad to function as an unambiguous style identity. The strongest historical evidence surfaced here concerns the development of Black South African jazz and swing-oriented urban band practices from the 1920s through the early 1950s, including Sophiatown. “African Jazz” and “Sophiatown jazz” may be more historically discriminating names for a specific profile; retain “Township Jazz” as a search alias only until musicians/historians review the naming.

**Grounded observations**
- Christopher Ballantine documents the early urban Black South African jazz-band and vaudeville tradition, showing how American jazz, local musical practices, performance venues, social classes, and historical conditions interacted; he explicitly cautions against simple one-to-one genre/social mappings ([“Concert and Dance,” *Popular Music*, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261143000004475)).
- South African History Online describes the evolution of Sophiatown and Alexandra urban music through American swing, local dance culture, and the emergence of new local idioms ([“Music and culture as forms of resistance”](https://sahistory.org.za/article/music-and-culture-forms-resistance)).
- Allen's Kwela thesis distinguishes the swing-influenced “African Jazz” of the 1940s from later Kwela and Mbaqanga developments, while also documenting connections among them ([*Pennywhistle Kwela*, 1993](https://hdl.handle.net/10413/9106)).

**Candidate discriminators to test**
- Big-band/small-combo and dance-band profiles must be separately described; do not assume one orchestration.
- The historical profile may combine swing-derived ensemble practice with local melodic, rhythmic, and repertory materials.
- Sectional improvisation, ensemble riffs, vocal-fronted dance arrangements, and cyclic local forms need to be measured by period and repertoire.

**Not inferred**
- Do not equate “Township Jazz” with all jazz made in townships, all South African jazz, or contemporary township jazz scenes.
- Do not create a single generic jazz preset from this label without a narrower historical scope.

**Next evidence gate:** consult South African jazz historians and performers; determine whether the catalog entry should be renamed to “Sophiatown African Jazz”, split into historical subprofiles, or retained as an umbrella node with narrower child styles.

### 160 — South African Bubblegum Pop

**Scope to investigate:** the locally developed Black South African dance-pop form commonly discussed as bubblegum or township pop in the 1980s, while keeping the international Euro-American bubblegum label distinct.

**Grounded observations**
- Eaby-Lomas explicitly discusses South African bubblegum as a local form shaped by mbaqanga history and Euro-American disco/house/pop, with energetic dance beats, township settings, programmed/sequenced electronic timbres, and vocal processing such as reverb, chorus, and echo ([“Kwaito as history,” *African Music*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.21504/amj.v12i1.2529)).
- The article identifies Brenda Fassie, The Soul Brothers, Yvonne Chaka Chaka, and Chicco Twala among important artists in the form, while also warning that artists and styles cross boundaries over time.
- Durbach's UNISA dissertation treats bubblegum/disco as one of the central popular genres of 1980s South Africa under apartheid and examines its political and industry context ([2015 dissertation](https://hdl.handle.net/10500/18725)).

**Candidate discriminators to test**
- Dance-oriented, bright/energetic beat structures with electronic or sequenced timbres.
- Studio production as part of the identity: synth timbres, programmed accompaniment, and vocal effects rather than an acoustic-band-only assumption.
- Local-language and township-life lyrical material alongside international pop/disco/house influence.
- Song/hook and dance priorities should be examined in recordings, not inferred as “lyrics unimportant”.

**Not inferred**
- Do not equate this with generic international bubblegum pop, synthpop, disco, or all 1980s South African popular music.
- Do not impose a fixed lyric language or political stance.

**Next evidence gate:** sample multiple artists/producers and period slices; document production techniques with consent/rights-cleared materials; distinguish bubblegum from crossover, disco, mbaqanga, and early Kwaito.

## 4. Relationship map: influence, not a single family tree

The sources support historical relationships, but not a simple deterministic chain:

- Marabi is a major early urban dance-music reference point for later South African popular music.
- African Jazz/Sophiatown band practices, Kwela, and Mbaqanga overlap historically and influence one another, but must remain distinguishable by period, groove, instrumentation, and arrangement.
- Bubblegum develops through Mbaqanga and international pop/disco/house interactions; Kwaito emerges later in a different post-apartheid context and reworks both local and international materials.
- Maskandi and Isicathamiya intersect with histories of migration, urban life, and Zulu performance, but their defining musical organizations are not derivative variants of the Marabi-to-Kwaito line.

For the eventual taxonomy graph, use typed, directional, time-scoped relations such as `historical_influence`, `shared_scene`, `adaptation_of`, `overlap_with`, and `distinct_from`. Do not encode all relations as undirected “related genres” edges. Every edge needs a source and a scope note.

## 5. Taxonomy decisions required before promotion

1. **Constellation name:** `SouthernAfricanUrbanFolkDance` is not a satisfactory umbrella for an a cappella choral practice, guitar song traditions, urban dance genres, and electronic pop. Consider a broader, less prescriptive name such as `SouthernAfricanPopularAndPerformanceTraditions`. Do not rename the enum until serialized-ID compatibility and catalog migration are addressed.
2. **Township Jazz:** require a naming decision. “Township Jazz” is too broad without a declared period/scene. A Sophiatown/African Jazz profile may be more falsifiable, while the existing display label can remain an alias pending review.
3. **Mbaqanga:** require period/usage metadata. Historical literature uses “African Jazz” and “Mbaqanga” in more than one way; the route must say which tradition/period it represents.
4. **Maskandi aliases:** store Maskandi/Maskanda and source-specific orthography as aliases, not as separate musical styles absent evidence.
5. **Bubblegum aliases:** keep “South African Bubblegum”, “South African Bubblegum Pop”, and “Township Pop” as scoped aliases; do not merge with the Euro-American genre automatically.
6. **No automatic authenticity promotion:** all eight remain expert-review-required until knowledgeable practitioners review the actual profile and rendered examples.

## 6. Proposed implementation gates

For each style, completion should mean all of the following, not just a registry row:

- **G0 — identity:** stable ID, canonical name, aliases, period/region/scene scope, sources, and a naming reviewer.
- **G1 — anatomy:** independently specified phrase/form, rhythmic organization, pitch/harmonic practice, melodic language, ensemble roles, vocal/performance practice, and production context where relevant. Mark unsupported axes `unknown`, never fill them with constellation defaults and present them as grounded facts.
- **G2 — counterexample:** fixtures that distinguish the style from its nearest neighbours (for example, Kwela vs. Mbaqanga; Marabi vs. African Jazz; Bubblegum vs. Kwaito; Isicathamiya vs. generic choral music).
- **G3 — executable behavior:** a callable route that changes the intended musical features through the production composer path, with target sensitivity and protected non-target invariance.
- **G4 — rendering:** renderer/instrument availability is reported separately. Missing pennywhistle, guitar articulation, choir blend, or period-specific synth sounds must be disclosed rather than silently replaced.
- **G5 — listening and expert review:** blinded family/style discrimination, within-style variation, practitioner review, and rights-cleared examples. A route or symbolic test alone cannot pass this gate.

Recommended measurements include groove-pattern precision/recall and phase stability; phrase/form obligation accuracy; melodic contour and ornament distributions; harmonic-cycle behavior where applicable; role-conditioned polyphonic independence; vocal range/part leading and response timing; production timbre/temporal-envelope descriptors; within-style diversity; and confusion against the nearest-neighbour styles. Each metric needs a source-appropriate reference set and a stated limitation.

## 7. Immediate next work

1. Obtain a small, rights-cleared, period-labelled reference set per tradition. Do not ingest or train on recordings/scores merely because they are publicly reachable.
2. Build a musician-reviewed annotation schema for each style rather than forcing one universal set of fields.
3. Run a pairwise confusion study on the nearest neighbours before adding any of the eight to the executable composer roster.
4. Start with Marabi, Kwela, and Mbaqanga for a historical urban-dance lineage pilot; pursue Maskandi and Isicathamiya as separate vocal/instrumental research tracks; then scope Bubblegum and Kwaito as period-specific production/song tracks. Township Jazz needs a naming decision first.
